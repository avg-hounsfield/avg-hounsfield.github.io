Acute Pancreatitis: which imaging to order

6 clinical scenarios from the ACR Appropriateness Criteria topic "Acute Pancreatitis", grouped by the imaging study ACR rates highest for each. Studies listed together share the same ACR category; ACR does not rank studies within a category.

Independent summary of the ACR Appropriateness Criteria as of November 2025. Not affiliated with or endorsed by the American College of Radiology. For education; confirm against the linked source and your institution's protocols.

Usually Appropriate MRI abdomen without and with IV contrast with MRCP; CT abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast

4 scenarios

Pancreatitis suspected, atypical presentation

Suspected acute pancreatitis. Initial presentation with atypical signs and symptoms; including equivocal amylase and lipase values (possibly confounded by acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease) and when diagnoses other than pancreatitis may be possible (bowel perforation, bowel ischemia, etc.). Initial imaging.

May be appropriate: US abdomen; US duplex Doppler abdomen; MRI abdomen without IV contrast with MRCP; CT abdomen and pelvis without IV contrast

ACR source for this scenario

Pancreatitis, acute, critically ill, >48 hours

Acute pancreatitis. Critically ill, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), severe clinical scores (eg, Acute Physiology, Age, and Chronic Health Evaluation [APACHE]-II, Bedside Index for Severity in AP [BISAP], or Marshall). Greater than 48 to 72 hours after onset of symptoms.

May be appropriate: US duplex Doppler abdomen; MRI abdomen without IV contrast with MRCP; CT abdomen and pelvis without IV contrast

ACR source for this scenario

Pancreatitis, acute, severe, > 7 days

Acute pancreatitis. Continued SIRS, severe clinical scores, leukocytosis, and fever. Greater than 7 to 21 days after onset of symptoms.

May be appropriate: US abdomen; US duplex Doppler abdomen; MRI abdomen without IV contrast with MRCP; CT abdomen and pelvis without IV contrast

ACR source for this scenario

Pancreatitis, persistent > 4 weeks

Acute pancreatitis. Known pancreatic or peripancreatic fluid collections with continued abdominal pain, early satiety, nausea, vomiting, or signs of infection. Greater than 4 weeks after symptom onset.

May be appropriate: US abdomen; US duplex Doppler abdomen; MRI abdomen without IV contrast with MRCP; CT abdomen and pelvis without IV contrast

ACR source for this scenario

Usually Appropriate CT abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast

1 scenario

Pancreatitis necrotizing, significant clinical deterioration

Known necrotizing pancreatitis. Significant deterioration in clinical status, including abrupt decrease in hemoglobin or hematocrit, hypotension, tachycardia, tachypnea, abrupt change in fever curve, or increase in white blood cells.

May be appropriate: US abdomen; US duplex Doppler abdomen; MRI abdomen without and with IV contrast with MRCP; MRI abdomen without IV contrast with MRCP; CT abdomen and pelvis without IV contrast; CT abdomen and pelvis without and with IV contrast

ACR source for this scenario

Usually Appropriate US abdomen

1 scenario

Pancreatitis suspected, typical presentation

Suspected acute pancreatitis. First time presentation. Epigastric pain and increased amylase and lipase. Less than 48 to 72 hours after symptom onset. Initial imaging.

May be appropriate: US duplex Doppler abdomen; MRI abdomen without and with IV contrast with MRCP; MRI abdomen without IV contrast with MRCP; CT abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast

ACR source for this scenario
